The Taro Tsujimoto Lab

An analytics and biomechanics project on hockey’s most famous nonexistent player, the Buffalo Sabres’ imaginary 11th-round pick in the 1974 NHL draft.

The legend

In 1974 the Sabres announced Taro Tsujimoto, a forward from the “Tokyo Katanas,” with the 183rd pick in the 11th round. Katana is a loose translation of “sabre,” and neither the player nor the team existed. Reports credit general manager Punch Imlach, with PR director Paul Wieland helping come up with the name, and Panini even issued a rookie card for him in 2010-11. One account gives him 15 goals and 25 points in his draft year, so that is the bar this lab measures against.

Unlike Sidd Finch, the legend gives Tsujimoto no signature skill. Every ability setting below is a hypothetical you choose.

Why the stick matters

A slap shot loads the stick by driving the blade into the ice, and the shaft bends and then springs back as the puck leaves. That stored energy is the stick flex link.

Puck versus blade

The puck leaves faster than the blade moves because the stick is much heavier than the puck. This page uses a factor of 1.25, which is an assumption.

Method and limits

Flight time is distance divided by speed, ignoring drag and the puck’s flight path. The save model is a logistic curve on the time left after the goalie’s reaction and a fixed 100 ms save movement, tuned so an 80 mph shot from 35 ft against a 180 ms goalie is stopped 91 percent of the time. Goal chance multiplies the on-net rate by the miss rate. Blocks, screens, deflections, rebounds, and shot placement are not modeled. The kinetic chain split and puck factor are teaching assumptions. Nothing here predicts anything, because Taro Tsujimoto is fiction.
